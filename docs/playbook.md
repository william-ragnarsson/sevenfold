# The SevenFold Rubric

> This is the exact framework I used to evaluate 800+ pitch decks during my year at Plug and Play Tech Center, one of the world's most active startup accelerators. Everything below is what I learned from weekly partner meetings, deal reviews, and pattern-matching across hundreds of decks, not generic advice you can Google. This is what the AI uses to evaluate your deck.

Most of the decks I reviewed were pre-seed or seed, and the rubric is calibrated for that stage. Little or no revenue, a small team and a product that's still rough are all normal this early. What isn't normal is a deck that hides them.

---

## 1. Team

**Team is the single most important criterion for funding - above product, above market, above traction.**

The core question: can I find these people online, and does what I find match what they're claiming?

### Discoverability
Every founder needs to be easily searchable via LinkedIn, GitHub, a personal site, publications, or anything that lets me verify who they are and what they've done. If I can't find a founder with a quick search, that's an immediate yellow flag. The more I can read into the founding team from public sources, the better. Include links in your deck; it removes friction and signals confidence.

### Complementarity
The best teams have founders whose skills don't overlap, typically a technical co-founder paired with a business or domain-expert co-founder. A team of all engineers or all business people raises questions about execution range.

### Full-time commitment
It's fine, and common, for founders to still be employed or studying at the start of the journey. What matters is the plan: if the funding round will allow them to go full-time, that's a reasonable answer. What I look for is that the founders have a clear, credible path to full commitment, not that they already are.

### Track record
Founders who have started a company before have already made the first-time mistakes. Prior startup experience counts even if that company failed, as long as the founder can say what they learned from it. A previous exit is a strong signal: it proves they can take something all the way. Neither is required, but if a founder has it, it belongs on the team slide, and it should check out online.

### History together
Co-founder breakups kill a lot of early startups. Founders who have worked together before, at a previous company, in a research group or on an earlier project, have already been tested under pressure. Founders who met recently aren't disqualified, but it's a risk, and the deck is stronger if it says how they know each other.

### Team size
- **Solo founders** carry every role and every decision alone, and the company has a single point of failure. It can work, but I want to see how the gaps get covered: early hires, committed advisors or an active co-founder search.
- **Too many co-founders**, four or five or more, usually means thin equity per founder, unclear decision-making, and co-founder titles handed out in place of salaries.
- Two or three founders with clearly split roles is the sweet spot.

## 2. Problem

**The best decks make me feel the problem before they show me the solution.**

### Painful and urgent
The problem should be a must-solve, not a nice-to-have. A good test: is someone already losing money, time or customers because of it, and do they know it? Problems people put up with don't get budgets.

### A specific customer
"SMEs" or "anyone who shops online" isn't a customer. I want to know exactly who has the problem: their role, their kind of company, and the moment they feel it. The more specific the persona, the more credible everything after it.

### How it's solved today
Every real problem is already being solved somehow: with spreadsheets, an agency, an incumbent tool, or by simply living with it. A deck that doesn't name the current workaround usually hasn't talked to enough customers.

### The cost of the status quo
Put a number on what the problem costs today, in hours, money or lost revenue. That number is what makes the product's price feel cheap, and it's the backbone of a believable business case.

---

## 3. Product

### Show it working
At pre-seed and seed I need evidence that the product exists: screenshots, a short demo video, or a link to try it. A deck that only describes the product in words makes me wonder whether it's been built yet.

### A company, not a feature
Could an existing platform ship this as a feature next quarter? If the whole product is one button inside a tool customers already use, the incumbent can copy it, and the startup lives at its mercy. I want a product that can grow into a company on its own.

### Thin AI wrappers
If the product is mostly a prompt on top of someone else's model, there's no moat: a competitor can rebuild it quickly, and the model provider can absorb it in its next release. I look for what's proprietary around the model: the startup's own data, deep integration into a workflow, or distribution others can't copy.

---

## 4. Market

### The "1% of a huge market" trap
"The market is $50B, and if we capture just 1% we're a $500M company" is a red flag, not a plan. It shows the market was sized from the top down, without working out who will actually buy. I'd rather see a smaller, bottom-up number: how many customers the company can realistically reach, times what each one pays.

---

## 5. Traction

**At this stage I don't expect much traction. I do expect the numbers that are there to be honest.**

### Revenue and growth
If there's revenue, show it together with the growth rate, month over month. Early on, the growth rate says more than the absolute number: small revenue growing fast beats bigger revenue that's flat.

### Retention and churn
Signing up customers is the easy part; keeping them is the proof. Customers who stay, keep paying and keep using the product are the strongest signal a deck can show. When sign-ups are front and center and retention is nowhere to be found, I start wondering why.

### Vanity metrics
Downloads, sign-ups, waitlist size, followers and impressions are easy to inflate and say little about whether anyone values the product. They're fine as supporting detail, never as the headline. Paying customers, active usage and retention are what count.

### Charts without axes or dates
A line going up and to the right means nothing without labelled axes and dates. Unlabelled charts, cumulative charts that hide a slowdown, and metrics with no "as of" date make me assume the real numbers are worse than they look.

---

## 6. Go-to-Market

### Proven channels
"Social media, partnerships and word of mouth" is not a go-to-market plan. I want at least one channel that has already brought in customers, a rough idea of what each customer cost, and a plan to scale that channel.

### Partnerships that are real
Logos on a partnerships slide often mean much less than they suggest: a cloud-credits program, an accelerator perk, or a single intro call. A real partnership has a signed agreement, a pilot or revenue behind it. If a big name is on the slide, the deck should say what the relationship actually is, and it should hold up when I check.

---

## 7. Competition

**There will always be competitors. Pretending otherwise destroys credibility.**

Include a competitors slide, and make it accurate. The goal isn't to prove you have no competition; it's to show you understand the landscape and know specifically why you win.

- **Too little competition** isn't always a green flag - it can mean the market doesn't exist yet or that smarter players already tried and failed.
- **Too much competition** signals a crowded space where differentiation is hard and margins get squeezed.
- The sweet spot is a clear competitive landscape where your positioning is defensible and specific.

What I want to see: a competitors slide that doesn't hide the real players, a clear axis of differentiation, and a convincing answer to "why you, why now."

---

## 8. Red Flags

### Buzzwords without substance
"AI-powered, blockchain-enabled platform for the future of work" tells me nothing. Every buzzword needs a concrete explanation of what the technology does and why it matters to the customer. The more buzzwords per slide, the less I believe there's something underneath.

### Founder controversies
I search every founder. Lawsuits, fraud allegations, a previous company that ended badly for its investors or customers, or public behaviour a partner would be embarrassed by: any of these needs a direct conversation before anything else, and some are deal-breakers. I make sure it's actually the same person before counting it, since a common name can turn up someone else's history. It's always better for the founder to raise it first than for me to find it.

---

## 9. Deck Basics

### Make it easy to keep reading
Include your website URL in the deck. It sounds small, but it signals that you're ready for scrutiny, and it removes the friction for anyone who wants to dig deeper immediately. A missing or broken website link is a subtle but real confidence knock.
